# A first listening session

Choose Listening guide in the All categories dropdown beneath Audition to find eight suggested starting scales. This fixed collection remains available when you change your Favorites. The outline bulb in the scale panel turns listening ideas on or off. It starts off; your choice is remembered across projects when you reopen the interface. With ideas enabled, Listening guide opens this text inside Inton. Browse guided scales opens the Library to the complete collection and clears any search filter.

For the first chord comparison, use a quiet sawtooth or another sound with clear harmonics, with chorus and unison detuning turned down. Small tuning changes can be difficult to hear with low sine tones. Hold notes long enough to hear their beating, then try the same material on a patch you would use in a piece. A tuning can suit one sound better than another.

## Reading the examples

The numbers below count keys above a starting note: 0 is your starting key, +1 is the very next key, including black keys. For 0–6–11, hold the starting key and keys six and eleven positions above it. If you start at MIDI 60, those are MIDI 60, 66 and 71. Black and white keys do not mark the familiar major-scale pattern in these tunings.

These examples use Inton’s factory mappings, with one successive MIDI key per scale degree. An imported keyboard map can arrange notes differently. Begin with chromatic note input and turn off a conventional major/minor note filter. Inton supplies the tuning; your instrument or controller supplies the notes.

## A familiar starting point

In 12 EDO, hold 0–4–7. Keep those keys down and compare Soft Circuit. Listen for a change in the pulsing between notes. These are small, fixed pitch-class offsets, so familiar twelve-note keyboard shapes still apply.

Next, compare Quarter-comma meantone with 12 EDO. Start on MIDI 60 and play 60–64–67. Listen to the major third, then move the whole chord one key higher. This temperament has different interval qualities in different keys; some chords settle more readily than others.

## 19 EDO: change one voice

Hold 0 and +11, a fifth approximation. Add +6 for a major third, then lower that middle voice to +5 for a minor third. Move it back and forth while the outer voices stay still. Listen to how much the chord changes with a single key movement.

Try a slow arpeggio of each shape. Repeat it nineteen keys higher to hear the octave. Once the shapes feel familiar, try a short phrase using offsets 0, 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17, 19. This selects seven degrees from the nineteen available; you can leave the others unused.

## 22 EDO: four shades of a third

Keep a root and +13 held. Move the middle voice through +5, +6, +7 and +8. Listen to four different thirds against the same outer notes. Sevish’s practical reference calls these subminor, minor, major and supermajor.

Return to 0–7–13, then add +18. This approximates harmonics 4:5:6:7. Listen especially to the seventh. For a wider chord, try adding +26 and +32, corresponding approximately to the ninth and eleventh harmonics above that same 4-based root. These examples and labels are documented in the reference below.

[Sevish’s 22-EDO chord reference](https://sevish.com/22edo/)

## 31 EDO: smaller movements

Begin with 0–10–18, a major triad approximation. Lower the middle note to +8 for a minor triad, then try +9 between them. Sustain the root and fifth while comparing the three positions. A single step is about 38.71 cents, so these movements are smaller than in 19 EDO.

Make a short phrase using the major and minor shapes. Then move just one chord tone by one key and listen again. Repeat shapes thirty-one keys higher for an octave. You do not need a phrase that uses all thirty-one degrees.

## 7 EDO: follow a melody

Try a drone with a melody using offsets 0, 1, 2 and 4. Repeat the melody seven keys higher. The steps are wider than in 12 EDO; listen to their spacing before trying to recreate familiar chords.

## Bohlen–Pierce: a different repeat

13 ED3 divides a 3:1 span into thirteen equal steps. That repeating interval is a tritave: an octave plus a fifth. There is no exact octave among its degrees.

Hold a drone and move a second voice one key at a time. Stop at intervals you want to hear longer. Add a third voice, or try 0–6–10, an approximation of 3:5:7. Repeat a short phrase thirteen keys higher to hear the tritave. Try a hollow-sounding patch as well as a bright one and compare how the intervals blend.

## Compare a phrase, then try morphing

Build a small Set with 12 EDO, 19 EDO, 22 EDO, 31 EDO and 13 ED3. First adapt your chord shapes for each tuning using the examples above. Then try keeping exactly the same MIDI phrase while switching slots. In that second experiment, both the interval sizes and the phrase’s overall span can change greatly: seven keys is a fifth in 12 EDO but much less than a fifth in 31 EDO.

Try a Morph Time of one to four seconds and move between two slots. Inton moves the frequency assigned to each MIDI note toward its new value. How smoothly held notes follow depends on the receiving instrument. Keep a transition you like and build a phrase around it.

## Pure ratios and detuned synth chords

Load 5-limit major and start on its root, MIDI 60. Offsets 0–2–4 form a pure major triad in this seven-note mapping. Compare that with the major shapes above. A pure major third is about 386.31 cents and a pure fifth about 701.96 cents.

For twelve-note experiments, compare Frayed Thirds and Restless Fifths with a sustained C–E–G chord. Frayed Thirds keeps C–G close to pure while widening the third. Restless Fifths keeps C–E close to pure while narrowing the fifth. Listen to where the pulsing comes from in each chord.

Carlos Alpha, Beta and Gamma offer another direction. Inton uses Wendy Carlos’s published rounded step sizes: 78.0, 63.8 and 35.1 cents. Try 0–5–9 in Alpha, 0–6–11 in Beta, or 0–11–20 in Gamma for major-triad approximations. These tunings have no exact octave.

## Sources and further listening

The sequence and listening exercises were written for Inton. The interval shapes were checked against the factory tunings. The 22-EDO chord labels and harmonic examples above follow Sevish’s own reference. This is not a course or recommendation endorsed by the linked musicians.

Marc Sabat’s introduction explains the distinction between octave divisions that approximate familiar ratio intervals and those that depart further from them. It also introduces non-octave divisions.

[Marc Sabat’s introduction to EDO tunings, hosted by Ableton](https://tuning.ableton.com/edo/intro-to-edo/)

Sevish’s scale impressions are personal listening reactions, rather than a curriculum. His resources page offers a tuning pack and links to tools. He also links Lumi’s analysis of the 22-EDO piece Gleam; that is a useful next stop for hearing these ideas in a composition.

[Sevish’s scale impressions](https://sevish.com/2022/sevishs-scale-impressions/)

[Sevish’s music resources](https://sevish.com/music-resources/)

[Lumi’s Gleam analysis, introduced by Sevish](https://sevish.com/2022/watch-lumis-music-theory-analysis-of-gleam/)

William Sethares’s book Tuning, Timbre, Spectrum, Scale explores why a sound’s spectrum affects which tunings and intervals blend with it. His site provides a chapter guide and sound examples.

[William Sethares’s book page](https://sethares.engr.wisc.edu/ttss.html)
